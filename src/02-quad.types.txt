@ quad | Quadratic Equations | 11 | Algebra

## If this type comes, solve like this
Q: If the question gives $\alpha^n+\beta^n$ (called $P_n$, $S_n$ or $a_n$) and asks for a ratio or combination of very high powers
R: You see $P_n=\alpha^n+\beta^n$ (or $\alpha^n-\beta^n$) with big indices like $P_{10}, P_{17}, a_{2024}$, and an expression such as $\frac{P_{17}P_{20}+kP_{17}P_{19}}{\ldots}$.
F: \alpha,\beta\text{ roots of }ax^2+bx+c=0\ \Rightarrow\ aS_{n+2}+bS_{n+1}+cS_n=0
S: Write the quadratic whose roots are $\alpha,\beta$. || Multiply $a\alpha^2+b\alpha+c=0$ by $\alpha^n$ and the same for $\beta$; adding gives the recurrence $aS_{n+2}+bS_{n+1}+cS_n=0$. || Look at the asked expression: group terms so they look like $aS_{n+2}+bS_{n+1}$ — that equals $-cS_n$. || Everything collapses to a simple multiple of one $S_k$; cancel and finish.
E: $\alpha,\beta$ roots of $x^2-x-1=0$, so $S_{n+2}=S_{n+1}+S_n$. Then $\frac{S_{10}-S_9}{S_8}=\frac{S_8}{S_8}=1$.
N: If $P_{10},P_9,P_8$ are given numerically, the recurrence $P_{10}=pP_9+qP_8$ tells you $p,q$, i.e. the quadratic itself.
H: Trap: do not try to expand $\alpha^{20}$; and for $\alpha^n-\beta^n$ the same recurrence still holds.
P: 01:1,17,28,31,33,53,3,56,21

Q: If it asks you to form a new quadratic whose roots are some function of $\alpha$ and $\beta$
R: "Find the equation whose roots are $\frac1\alpha,\frac1\beta$" or "$\alpha^4+\beta^4$ and $\frac{1}{10}(\alpha^6+\beta^6)$" or "$\frac{3\alpha}{\beta}$ and $\frac{4\alpha}{\gamma}$".
F: x^2-(\text{sum of new roots})\,x+(\text{product of new roots})=0
S: From the given equation write $\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba$ and $\alpha\beta=\frac ca$. || Express the sum and the product of the new roots in terms of $\alpha+\beta$ and $\alpha\beta$ (use $\alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta$ etc.). || Write $x^2-(\text{sum})x+\text{product}=0$. || Shortcut for simple maps: roots $\frac1\alpha$ → replace $x$ by $\frac1x$; roots $k\alpha$ → replace $x$ by $\frac xk$; roots $\alpha+k$ → replace $x$ by $x-k$.
E: $x^2-3x+2=0$ has roots $1,2$. Roots doubled: replace $x\to\frac x2$: $\frac{x^2}4-\frac{3x}2+2=0\Rightarrow x^2-6x+8=0$ (roots $2,4$ ✓).
P: 01:2,7,8,19,32,24,30,50,55

Q: If the roots must lie in an interval, both be greater/less than a number, or have the same sign
R: Phrases like "both roots lie in $[1,5]$", "one root in $(0,2)$ and the other in $(2,3)$", "both roots positive", "$k$ lies between the roots".
F: \text{Conditions use } D,\ \ af(k),\ \ \text{vertex } x=-\tfrac b{2a}
S: Sketch the parabola $y=f(x)$ (opening up if $a>0$). || Both roots in $(p,q)$: $D\ge0$, $af(p)>0$, $af(q)>0$, $p<-\frac b{2a}<q$. || One root in $(p,q)$ only: $f(p)\,f(q)<0$. || $k$ between the roots: $af(k)<0$ (no need to check $D$). || Both positive: $D\ge0$, sum $>0$, product $>0$. || Intersect all conditions and count the allowed parameter values.
E: Roots of $x^2-mx+4=0$ both in $[1,5]$: $D=m^2-16\ge0$, $f(1)=5-m\ge0$, $f(5)=29-5m\ge0$, $1\le\frac m2\le5$ ⇒ $m\in[4,5]$.
H: Trap: if the leading coefficient contains the parameter, multiply by $a$ (use $af(k)$), and check the case $a=0$ separately.
P: 01:4,5,12,20,52,60

Q: If two quadratics have a common root
R: Two equations are given, with sentences like "$\alpha,\beta$ are roots of the first and $\alpha,\gamma$ are roots of the second".
F: \text{Common root: subtract the equations (after equalising the } x^2 \text{ coefficients)}
S: Make the $x^2$ coefficients equal and subtract: the $x^2$ terms cancel, giving a linear equation that gives the common root $\alpha$. || Put $\alpha$ back in one equation to get the parameter. || Use sum/product of roots in each equation to get the other roots $\beta$, $\gamma$. || Answer the asked combination.
E: $x^2-x+2\lambda=0$ and $3x^2-10x+27\lambda=0$ share $\alpha$. $3\times$first − second: $7x-21\lambda=0\Rightarrow\alpha=3\lambda$; put in the first: $9\lambda^2-3\lambda+2\lambda=0\Rightarrow\lambda=\frac19$.
H: Trap: "both roots common" means the coefficients are proportional — a different condition.
P: 01:18,19,41,42,43,54

Q: If the equation is in $e^x$, $a^x$, $(\sqrt3+\sqrt2)^x$, $9^{\tan^2x}$ etc. and asks for the number or sum of real solutions
R: Exponentials with the same base appear in powers like $e^{4x},e^{3x},e^{2x},e^x$, or pairs whose product is $1$ like $(\sqrt3+\sqrt2)^x$ and $(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)^x$.
F: t=e^x>0;\qquad(\sqrt3+\sqrt2)(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)=1\Rightarrow t+\tfrac1t
S: Substitute $t=a^x$ (always $t>0$). || If the polynomial in $t$ is palindromic (coefficients read the same both ways), divide by $t^2$ and put $u=t+\frac1t$ ($u\ge2$ for $t>0$). || Solve for $u$, then $t$; keep only positive $t$. || Each valid $t$ gives exactly one $x=\ln t$; count them (or add the $x$'s: $x_1+x_2=\ln(t_1t_2)$).
E: $(\sqrt3+\sqrt2)^x+(\sqrt3-\sqrt2)^x=10$: $t+\frac1t=10\Rightarrow t=5\pm2\sqrt6=(\sqrt3\pm\sqrt2)^2$, so $x=\pm2$: two solutions.
H: Trap: when the exponent is $x^2-4$ instead of $x$, each value of the exponent gives two values of $x$.
P: 01:10,15,25,38,44,46,27,45,59,61,37

Q: If a quadratic expression with a parameter must be always positive, or must have real/equal roots
R: "is positive for all $x\in\mathbb R$", "has equal roots", "has real roots", with the parameter inside the coefficients.
F: ax^2+bx+c>0\ \forall x\iff a>0,\ D<0;\qquad\text{equal roots}\iff D=0
S: Identify $a,b,c$ in terms of the parameter. || Write the needed conditions on $a$ and $D$. || Solve the inequalities and intersect. || Count integer values if asked; check $a=0$ separately (then it is linear).
E: $(1+2m)x^2-2(1+3m)x+4(1+m)>0\ \forall x$: need $1+2m>0$ and $4(1+3m)^2-16(1+2m)(1+m)<0\Rightarrow m^2-6m-3<0$.
N: For $a(b-c)x^2+b(c-a)x+c(a-b)=0$, $x=1$ is always a root (coefficients add to 0). Equal roots then means both roots are 1.
P: 01:11,22,47,40

Q: If it asks the range of $y=\frac{a_1x^2+b_1x+c_1}{a_2x^2+b_2x+c_2}$
R: A function defined as a ratio of two quadratics, and "range of $f$ is…".
F: y(a_2x^2+b_2x+c_2)=a_1x^2+b_1x+c_1\ \Rightarrow\ D\ge0
S: Put $y=f(x)$ and cross-multiply. || Collect as a quadratic in $x$: $(a_2y-a_1)x^2+(b_2y-b_1)x+(c_2y-c_1)=0$. || For real $x$ need discriminant $\ge0$; solve this inequality in $y$. || Check $y$ values that would need an excluded $x$ (zeros of the denominator) and the case where the $x^2$ coefficient vanishes.
P: 01:9

Q: If the roots must be integers or rational numbers
R: "integral roots", "rational roots", "$\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb N$", or "if $2-\sqrt3$ is a root and $p,q\in\mathbb Q$".
F: \text{rational roots}\iff D\ \text{is a perfect square};\quad\text{irrational roots come in pairs } p\pm\sqrt q
S: For integer/rational roots, write $D$ and demand it be a perfect square; list the possibilities. || For a root like $2-\sqrt3$ with rational coefficients, the other root is $2+\sqrt3$; use sum and product. || For natural-number roots with a given sum, list factor pairs of the product.
E: $x^2+4x-n=0$ has integer roots iff $16+4n$ is a perfect square, i.e. $n+4$ is a perfect square.
P: 01:35,26,57,58

Q: If it asks a symmetric value like $a^4+b^4+c^4$ from $a+b+c$, $ab+bc+ca$, $abc$ (cubic roots)
R: Three numbers (or three roots of a cubic) and the values of their elementary sums are given.
F: p_1=e_1,\ p_2=e_1p_1-2e_2,\ p_3=e_1p_2-e_2p_1+3e_3,\ p_4=e_1p_3-e_2p_2+e_3p_1
S: Write $e_1=a+b+c$, $e_2=ab+bc+ca$, $e_3=abc$. || Compute $p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4$ (power sums) one after the other with Newton's identities. || Substitute the numbers.
E: $e_1=1,e_2=2,e_3=3$: $p_2=1-4=-3$, $p_3=-3-2+9=4$, $p_4=4+6+3=13$.
P: 01:34,36

Q: If a log, surd or modulus equation turns into a quadratic
R: Logs with swapped bases ($\log_{(x+1)}\ldots$ and $\log_{(2x+5)}\ldots$), or $\sqrt x$ with $x$, or $|\sqrt x-3|$.
F: \log_ab\cdot\log_ba=1;\qquad t=\sqrt x\ \text{or}\ t=|x|
S: Factor the log arguments so the same two bases appear. || Put $t=\log_ab$; the other log becomes $\frac1t$; solve the quadratic in $t$. || For surds, put $t=\sqrt x\ge0$. || Check the domain at the end.
P: 01:14,6

Q: If the value of an infinite continued fraction is asked
R: An expression like $4+\frac1{5+\frac1{4+\frac1{5+\cdots}}}$.
F: y=a+\frac1{b+\frac1y}
S: Call the whole value $y$. || After one full period ($a$, then $b$) the pattern repeats, so the tail equals $y$. || Simplify to a quadratic in $y$ and take the positive root.
E: $y=4+\frac1{5+\frac1y}=4+\frac{y}{5y+1}\Rightarrow5y^2-20y-4=0\Rightarrow y=2+\frac{2\sqrt{30}}5$.
P: 01:39

Q: Less common forms (shifted sums, root maps, polynomial composition)
R: Equations built from sums like $x(x+2)+(x+1)(x+3)+\cdots$, or "whenever $\alpha$ is a root so is $\alpha^2-2$", or $(x^2-9x+11)^2-(x-4)(x-5)=3$.
F: \text{use } \sum k,\ \sum k^2;\ \ \text{substitute a common block } u
S: Expand sums using $\sum k=\frac{n(n+1)}2$ and $\sum k^2$ to get an ordinary quadratic. || For root maps, roots must be fixed points of the map or form a 2-cycle; list the cases. || For composite forms, put $u=$ the repeated block and solve in $u$ first.
P: 01:49,48,23,16,13,62,51,29
