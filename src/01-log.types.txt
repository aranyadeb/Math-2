## If this type comes, solve like this
Q: If the equation has modulus terms like $|x-a|$, $|x+b|$ or $x|x+a|$ and asks for roots, their number or their sum
R: You see one or more absolute-value bars around linear expressions in $x$, and the question asks "number of real roots", "sum of roots" or "sum of squares of roots".
F: |u|=\begin{cases}u,&u\ge0\\-u,&u<0\end{cases}
S: Find the critical points: the values of $x$ that make each expression inside $|\ |$ zero. || Mark them on the number line; they split it into intervals. || In each interval remove the bars with the correct sign and solve the resulting (usually quadratic) equation. || Keep a root only if it lies inside the interval you were working in. || If the whole equation depends only on $|x-a|$, put $t=|x-a|\ge0$ instead and solve in $t$ directly.
E: $|x-2|^2+|x-2|-2=0$. Put $t=|x-2|\ge0$: $t^2+t-2=0\Rightarrow t=1$ (reject $t=-2$). So $|x-2|=1$, i.e. $x=1$ or $3$.
H: Trap: forgetting to check that each root belongs to its interval, or keeping a negative value of $t=|\ldots|$.
P: 00:1,2,5,6,10,14,18

Q: If it gives $|f|=|g|+|h|$ type equation where the right side's inside terms add to the left side's inside
R: The expression inside the left modulus equals the sum (or difference) of the expressions inside the right moduli, e.g. $|x^2+x-9|=|x|+|x^2-9|$.
F: |a+b|=|a|+|b|\iff ab\ge0;\qquad|a-b|=|a|+|b|\iff ab\le0
S: Name $a$ and $b$ so that the left side is $|a+b|$ (or $|a-b|$). || Replace the whole equation by the sign condition $ab\ge0$ (or $\le0$). || Solve that inequality with the wavy-curve method. || Read off the answer intervals.
E: $|x^2+x-9|=|x|+|x^2-9|$: here $a=x$, $b=x^2-9$, so we need $x(x^2-9)\ge0\Rightarrow x\in[-3,0]\cup[3,\infty)$.
P: 00:3

Q: If it is a logarithmic equation or inequality, especially with variable base or variable argument
R: $\log$ appears with $x$ in the base or the argument, often two logs whose bases and arguments swap (like $\log_{(x+1)}(\ldots)$ and $\log_{(2x+3)}(\ldots)$).
F: \log_ab=\frac{\ln b}{\ln a},\quad\log_ab\cdot\log_ba=1,\quad\log_{f}g>0\iff(f-1)(g-1)>0
S: Write the domain first: every argument $>0$, every base $>0$ and $\ne1$. || Factor the arguments; often they share the bases, e.g. $2x^2+5x+3=(x+1)(2x+3)$. || Use $\log_a(bc)=\log_ab+\log_ac$ so that $\log_ab$ and $\log_ba$ appear; put $t=\log_ab$, then $\log_ba=\frac1t$. || Solve the equation in $t$, go back to $x$. || Intersect with the domain; for inequalities remember the direction flips when the base is between $0$ and $1$.
E: $\log_4(x-1)=\log_2(x-3)$. Domain $x>3$. LHS $=\frac12\log_2(x-1)$, so $x-1=(x-3)^2\Rightarrow x^2-7x+10=0\Rightarrow x=2,5$. Only $x=5$ is in the domain: one solution.
H: Trap: $\log(x^2)=2\log|x|$, not $2\log x$; and always check the base $\ne1$.
P: 00:4,7,9,16

Q: If a rational expression must be positive (or negative) for every real $x$
R: "for all $x\in\mathbb R$" with a fraction of two quadratics, and a parameter $a$ in the numerator.
F: ax^2+bx+c<0\ \forall x\iff a<0\ \text{and}\ D<0
S: Check the sign of the denominator: if its discriminant is negative and leading coefficient positive, it is always positive. || Then the fraction has the same sign as the numerator. || Apply the "always negative/positive" condition to the numerator: leading coefficient sign and $D<0$. || Solve for the parameter and count the required values.
E: $\frac{ax^2+2(a+1)x+9a+4}{x^2-8x+32}<0\ \forall x$: denominator has $D=64-128<0$, so need $a<0$ and $4(a+1)^2-4a(9a+4)<0$.
P: 00:8

Q: If it is an exponential equation that becomes a quadratic in $a^x$
R: Terms like $8^{2x}$ and $8^x$, or $e^{2x}$ and $e^x$, or $2^{(\ldots)}=1$.
F: t=a^x>0,\qquad a^{f(x)}=1\iff f(x)=0
S: Substitute $t=a^x$ (so $a^{2x}=t^2$). || Solve the quadratic in $t$. || Reject any $t\le0$. || Convert back: $x=\log_at$. For "sum of solutions": $x_1+x_2=\log_a(t_1t_2)$.
E: $8^{2x}-16\cdot8^x+48=0$: $t^2-16t+48=0$, $t=4,12$. Sum of $x$ $=\log_8(4\cdot12)=\log_848$.
P: 00:15,13

Q: If it is a radical equation like $\sqrt A+\sqrt B=\sqrt C$
R: Square roots of quadratics on both sides; the question asks for the number of real roots.
F: \text{domain: all radicands}\ge0
S: Write the domain from every radicand. || Look for a common factor: often $A$, $B$, $C$ share a linear factor, which gives one root immediately. || Otherwise square, isolate the remaining root, square again. || Check every root in the original equation and in the domain.
H: Trap: squaring creates extra roots — always substitute back.
P: 00:17

Q: If it is a continued fraction, or an integer (Diophantine) equation
R: An expression that repeats forever like $3+\frac1{4+\frac1{3+\cdots}}$, or "$x,y\in\mathbb N$" with an equation such as $x^2-2^y=2023$.
F: y=a+\frac1{b+\frac1y}
S: Continued fraction: call the whole value $y$; the part after one full period is $y$ again. Form the quadratic and take the positive root. || Integer equation: check parity (odd/even), small values of the exponent, and factor differences of squares.
E: $y=3+\frac1{4+\frac1y}\Rightarrow y=3+\frac{y}{4y+1}\Rightarrow4y^2-12y-3=0\Rightarrow y=\frac{3+2\sqrt3}2$.
P: 00:11,19,12
